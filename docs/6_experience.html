Development of visual function

The visual system is a useful model for understanding how development and experience interact to build neural function.

Many visual pathways are already organized before normal vision begins.

But the ability to see with adult precision develops gradually and depends on activity, sensory experience and circuit maturation.

Anatomy of the eye and retina

Light enters through the cornea and lens and is focused onto the retina.

The retina is a specialized part of the nervous system that converts light into neural signals.

  • Fovea: highest visual acuity
  • Optic nerve: carries retinal output to the brain

Vision begins with the transduction of light into neural activity in the retina.

The retina performs neural computations

The retina is not simply a light detector: it is a layered neural circuit that begins processing visual information before it reaches the brain.

  • Photoreceptors convert light into electrical signals (cones and rods ).
  • Bipolar cells transmit signals through the retina.
  • Horizontal cells integrate activity across neighboring photoreceptors.
  • Amacrine cells shape temporal and spatial responses.
  • Retinal ganglion cells generate action potentials and form the optic nerve.

From the retina to the visual cortex

Retinal ganglion cells provide the output of the retina.

Their axons:

  • form the optic nerve
  • partially cross at the optic chiasm
  • reach the LGN

The LGN is a nucleus of the thalamus and acts as the major relay between the retina and visual cortex.

From the LGN, thalamocortical projections carry visual information to the V1.

How is visual information processed?

A fundamental concept in sensory systems is the receptive field. The receptive field of a neuron is the region of sensory space in which a stimulus can modify its activity.

In the retina, signals from many photoreceptors are integrated onto a much smaller number of retinal ganglion cells.

This convergence means that each ganglion cell integrates information from a region of the retina: its receptive field.

Two major organizations are:

  • Concentric ON-center / OFF-surround
  • Concentric OFF-center / ON-surround

Visual signals are segregated in the LGN

After leaving the retina, axons of retinal ganglion cells form the optic nerve.

At the optic chiasm:

  • fibers from the nasal retina cross
  • fibers from the temporal retina remain ipsilateral

The signals then reach the LGN of the thalamus.

Inside the LGN, information from the two eyes remains segregated into separate layers.

Each LGN neuron therefore receives its main retinal input from one eye.

LGN neurons retain concentric receptive fields similar to those of retinal ganglion cells:

ON-center / OFF-surround or OFF-center / ON-surround.

The signals from the two eyes are still largely separate at this stage of the visual pathway.

From eye segregation to binocular integration

In the LGN, signals from the two eyes remain segregated and organized in ocular dominance columns.

When these projections reach V1, they initially terminate in eye-specific territories.

Thus, information arriving from the two eyes is still anatomically separated at the first cortical input stage.

Within the visual cortex, however, cortical connections begin to combine information from the two eyes.

V1 neurons can therefore be:

  • monocular contralateral
  • monocular ipsilateral
  • binocular

Binocular neurons respond to stimulation of either eye, although one eye may dominate the response.

Across the population there is usually a contralateral-eye bias: neurons respond more to the eye opposite the recorded hemisphere.

Receptive fields change in the visual cortex

Retinal ganglion cells and LGN neurons have approximately circular center-surround receptive fields.

In V1, many neurons instead respond best to an elongated region of visual space.

Because of this elongated organization, many cortical neurons become selective for the orientation of an edge or bar.

A vertical receptive field, for example, responds strongly to a vertical edge but much less to the same edge rotated away from its preferred orientation.

As visual information moves into cortex, the level of abstraction increases: neurons begin to represent features such as orientation, rather than simply local changes in luminance.

How can an elongated receptive field emerge?

Circular LGN receptive fields → spatially aligned convergence → elongated cortical receptive field → orientation selectivity

A classical model proposed by Hubel and Wiesel explains cortical orientation selectivity through convergence.

Several LGN neurons with circular center-surround receptive fields project onto the same cortical neuron.

If those LGN receptive fields are aligned along a common axis, their combined input creates an elongated receptive field.

The cortical neuron therefore responds preferentially when a bar or edge activates those inputs simultaneously.

Classical feedforward model: Hubel & Wiesel 1962

Two major streams of visual processing

After V1, visual information is distributed through at least two major cortical processing streams. As visual signals move beyond V1, the level of abstraction continues to increase: different cortical networks extract increasingly complex information about objects, space, and actions.

Dorsal stream

Projects mainly toward the parietal cortex.

Processes information related to:

  • motion
  • spatial position
  • visually guided action

Often summarized as the “where / how” pathway.

Ventral stream

Projects mainly toward the temporal cortex.

Processes information related to:

  • shape
  • color
  • object identity

Often summarized as the “what” pathway.

Two major streams of visual processing

Visual acuity

Visual acuity is the ability to resolve fine spatial detail. Two nearby points can be distinguished only if the visual system preserves enough spatial information to represent them separately.

Acuity is highest at the fovea and decreases toward the peripheral visual field.

High acuity depends on several levels of the visual pathway:

  • dense spacing of photoreceptors in the fovea
  • low convergence from photoreceptors to RGC
  • small receptive fields

Spatial frequency

Spatial frequency describes how rapidly luminance changes across space.

For a grating, it is usually expressed as cycles per degree (c/deg) of visual angle.

The higher the spatial frequency, the finer the spatial detail that the visual system must resolve.

Contrast sensitivity

Contrast sensitivity is the ability to detect differences in luminance. A stimulus may be large enough to resolve but still invisible if its contrast is too low.

Contrast sensitivity depends strongly on spatial frequency: Human vision is usually most sensitive to intermediate spatial frequencies.

The contrast sensitivity function (CSF) describes the minimum contrast needed to detect gratings of different spatial frequencies.

Visual acuity mainly describes the finest resolvable detail, whereas contrast sensitivity describes visual performance across a much wider range of spatial scales.

Depth perception

Binocular cues (near)

Use information provided by the two eyes. The most important is retinal disparity: because the eyes occupy slightly different positions, the image projected onto each retina is not identical.

The visual system compares these two images to estimate depth.

Depth perception is therefore reconstructed from several visual cues rather than directly measured by a single receptor.

Depth perception

Monocular cues (far)

Depth can also be estimated from information available to one eye alone.

These cues are particularly important for objects that are farther away, where binocular disparity becomes small.

Depth perception is therefore reconstructed from several visual cues rather than directly measured by a single receptor.

Monocular cues to depth

The visual system can infer three-dimensional structure from patterns contained in a single retinal image.

Perspective

Parallel lines converge with distance and objects become progressively smaller in the retinal image.

Occlusion

If one object blocks part of another, the blocking object is interpreted as being closer.

Relative size

For objects of similar expected size, the object producing the smaller retinal image is generally interpreted as farther away.

Shading

The distribution of light and shadow provides information about three-dimensional shape.

The same pattern can appear convex or concave depending on the assumed direction of illumination.

The visual system at birth

Anatomically advanced

  • major visual pathways are present
  • retina and dLGN are organized
  • thalamocortical projections have reached V1
  • substantial cortical organization is already present

Functionally immature

  • low visual acuity
  • low contrast sensitivity
  • immature focusing
  • immature control of eye movements

A circuit can be present before it performs at the adult level.

Development of visual acuity

At birth, visual acuity is approximately 40 times poorer than in adults. Its development is limited by both peripheral and central maturation.

Peripheral factors

The retina is still immature at birth.

A major limitation is the maturation of photoreceptors, especially in the fovea:

  • photoreceptors become more densely packed
  • outer segments elongate
  • retinal sampling becomes more precise

Central factors

Visual acuity also depends on maturation of the LGN and V1.

During development:

  • inhibitory circuits mature
  • receptive fields become smaller and more selective
  • cortical processing becomes more spatially precise

The increase in visual acuity during development reflects the combined maturation of the retina, thalamus, and visual cortex.

Visual acuity progressively matures during childhood

Visual acuity improves very rapidly during the first year of life, but development continues for several years.

By approximately 5-6 years of age, high-contrast visual acuity approaches adult-like values, although the exact age depends on the task and measurement used.

The developmental improvement reflects the progressive maturation of the entire visual pathway:

retina -> LGN -> V1

Photoreceptors mature after birth

By approximately 4-6 years of age, foveal cone density and morphology approach adult values.

During development, especially in the fovea, photoreceptors:

  • become smaller and more densely packed
  • elongate their outer segments
  • become more regularly organized

This increases the number of photoreceptors sampling a given region of visual space.

Denser photoreceptor packing provides finer spatial sampling of the retinal image and contributes to the developmental improvement in visual acuity.

Neural activity begins before vision

Before normal visual experience begins, the immature retina is already electrically active.

Groups of neighboring retinal ganglion cells become active together in spontaneous patterns called retinal waves.

These waves propagate through developing visual pathways and provide patterned activity before the animal can use vision normally.

Mooney et al., 1996

Retinal waves provide structured information

Nearby retinal neurons tend to be active at similar times.

Their activity is therefore correlated in space and time.

Activity from different retinal regions is less strongly correlated.

This creates information about which neurons belong together even before patterned vision.

Spontaneous activity can therefore contribute to refinement of retinotopic and eye-specific connections before sensory experience begins.

Retinal activity helps organize thalamic connections

If spontaneous activity in one eye is blocked with TTX, the normal eye-specific projection from that retina to the LGN fails to develop correctly.

In the normal condition, inputs from the two eyes form distinct territories in the thalamus.

After TTX injection into one eye, the territory normally occupied by that eye is strongly reduced or can disappear.

Spontaneous retinal activity is therefore required for normal refinement and segregation of retinogeniculate connections.

Contrast sensitivity develops during childhood

During development, the contrast sensitivity function progressively changes:

  • sensitivity to contrast increases
  • progressively higher spatial frequencies become detectable
  • the range of spatial frequencies processed efficiently becomes broader

Development does not simply increase visual acuity: it improves sensitivity across the entire range of spatial frequencies.

The maturation of contrast sensitivity depends on the development of inhibitory circuitry in V1. Inhibition does not simply suppress visual activity: it refines the representation, helping cortical neurons discriminate contrast more precisely.

If wiring begins before vision, what is experience for?

Molecular guidance and spontaneous activity can establish a surprisingly organized initial circuit.

But normal visual experience is required to refine, stabilize and calibrate that circuit for the statistics of the real visual world.

This is where developmental neuroplasticity becomes especially important.

Critical periods

A critical period is a developmental window during which particular neural circuits are especially sensitive to experience.

Experience during this period can produce large and persistent changes in circuit organization and function.

The same manipulation later in life usually produces a much smaller effect.

The end of a critical period does not mean that plasticity disappears completely. It means that the rules and magnitude of plasticity change with maturation.

Human evidence: congenital cataract

A dense congenital cataract prevents patterned visual information from reaching the retina normally during early development.

Removing the cataract early generally allows substantially better visual development than restoring patterned vision only after prolonged deprivation.

The outcome depends not only on whether visual input is restored, but also on when it is restored.

Case study: S.B.

S.B. experienced severe visual deprivation from early life (3 years old) and underwent surgery only in adulthood, at about 50 years of age.

After sight was restored:

  • visual acuity improved, but remained far below normal
  • recognition of novel objects remained difficult
  • stereopsis did not develop
  • face perception was impaired
  • perception of complex forms remained strongly limited

Restoring visual input in adulthood was not sufficient to produce normal visual perception.

Some visual abilities require appropriate sensory experience during development.

Amblyopia: abnormal experience changes cortical function

Amblyopia is reduced visual function caused by abnormal visual experience during development, not simply by damage to the eye or retina.

Common developmental causes include:

  • unilateral cataract
  • strabismus
  • strong unequal refractive error between the eyes

If the two eyes provide persistently unequal or mismatched input, cortical circuits become biased toward the more effective input.

Hubel and Wiesel: monocular deprivation

A classic test of experience-dependent development

Manipulation

One eye of a young animal is closed during a period of early visual development.

Measurement

After deprivation, responses of individual neurons in V1 are measured separately through each eye.

Result

Many cortical neurons become dominated by the nondeprived eye and respond weakly or not at all to the deprived eye.

Wiesel & Hubel, 1963

Ocular dominance in normal visual cortex

Many neurons in binocular visual cortex can be driven by both eyes, although individual neurons may prefer one eye.

In species such as cats and primates, geniculocortical inputs from the two eyes form alternating ocular dominance columns, particularly in layer IV of V1.

Normal binocular vision therefore depends on coordinated convergence of the two eyes onto cortical circuits.

Monocular deprivation changes cortical responses

Before deprivation, V1 contains neurons spanning a broad range of ocular preferences, including many binocular neurons.

After deprivation during the critical period, the distribution shifts dramatically toward the open eye.

Monocular deprivation changes cortical responses

Monocular deprivation changes cortical responses

Functional change becomes anatomical change

After prolonged monocular deprivation, the shift in ocular dominance is accompanied by structural reorganization of the visual pathway.

The ocular dominance columns representing the deprived eye become smaller, while columns driven by the open eye expand.

At the same time, geniculocortical axon arbors serving the deprived eye shrink, whereas those serving the non-deprived eye occupy more cortical territory.

Prolonged abnormal visual experience can therefore transform an initial functional imbalance into a stable anatomical reorganization of developing visual cortex.

Why is monocular deprivation so powerful?

The effect is not explained simply by a lack of visual stimulation.

During monocular deprivation, the two eyes compete under strongly unequal conditions:

open eye -> effective patterned activity

deprived eye -> weak or abnormal drive

Strabismus reveals another rule

In strabismus, both eyes can remain active at the same level, but the two retinal images are spatially misaligned.

Activity from corresponding points in the two eyes is therefore less consistently correlated.

The result is a strong reduction in binocular cortical neurons and impaired stereoscopic vision.

Normal binocular development requires coordinated activity from the two eyes.

Strabismus reveals another rule

Correlation matters, not activity alone

Controlling the temporal relationship between the two eyes

Question

Is activity sufficient, or must activity from the two eyes be temporally coordinated?

Manipulation

Retinal activity is experimentally controlled so that the two optic pathways are activated either synchronously or asynchronously.

Result

Synchronous activity favors binocular convergence, whereas strongly asynchronous activity favors more monocular organization.

The temporal relationship between inputs provides information about which connections should be associated.

Critical-period plasticity changes with age

The response to the same sensory manipulation depends strongly on developmental age.

During the critical period, brief monocular deprivation can produce a large ocular-dominance shift.

Before the window is fully open or after it has largely closed, the same deprivation produces a smaller effect.

Critical periods therefore reflect developmental regulation of the capacity for experience-dependent circuit change.

Sensory experience helps regulate the window

The timing of critical-period closure is not determined by chronological age alone.

In animal models, rearing in darkness can delay aspects of visual cortical maturation and prolong the period during which strong experience-dependent plasticity can be expressed.

Experience is both a driver of plasticity and one of the signals that contributes to circuit maturation.

Inhibition helps open and regulate critical-period plasticity

One of the major developmental changes in visual cortex is maturation of GABAergic inhibition.

Experiments manipulating GABAergic transmission showed that a sufficient level of cortical inhibition is required for normal ocular-dominance plasticity to emerge.

The critical period is therefore not simply a time when the cortex is “more excitable”. It depends on maturation of a specific excitatory-inhibitory circuit state.

Hensch et al., 1998 | Fagiolini & Hensch, 2000

BDNF can change the timing of the critical period

BDNF promotes maturation of cortical inhibitory circuitry during development.

When BDNF expression is increased precociously in mouse visual cortex:

  • GABAergic maturation occurs earlier
  • visual functions mature earlier
  • susceptibility to monocular deprivation shifts to an earlier age

Changing circuit maturation can therefore shift the timing of developmental plasticity.

Huang et al., 1999

The mature circuit becomes harder to modify

Critical-period closure also involves the accumulation of molecular and structural constraints that stabilize mature circuits.

Perineuronal nets (PNNs) are extracellular-matrix structures that accumulate around subsets of neurons, particularly many parvalbumin interneurons.

These structures are associated with increased circuit stability and reduced juvenile-like plasticity.

Removing a structural brake can restore plasticity

Reopening ocular-dominance plasticity in adult visual cortex

Manipulation

Chondroitinase ABC is used to degrade chondroitin-sulfate proteoglycans in the mature extracellular matrix.

Test

Adult animals then undergo monocular deprivation, which normally produces little ocular-dominance shift after the critical period.

Result

Ocular-dominance plasticity reappears in the adult visual cortex.

Critical-period closure is therefore partly an actively maintained stable state, not simply the irreversible disappearance of plasticity.

Pizzorusso et al., 2002

Is sensory experience instructive?

Does sensory experience merely maintain circuits that are already specified, or can the identity of the input help instruct what a cortical area becomes?

In developing ferrets, retinal projections can be experimentally redirected toward the auditory thalamus.

Neurons in auditory cortex then develop visual response properties, including organized visual receptive fields.

The experiment shows that sensory input can instruct important functional properties of developing cortex, although cortical identity is not determined by sensory input alone.

Sur, Garraghty & Roe, 1988 | Roe et al., 1990

Development is a sequence of constraints and experience

Before vision

Molecular cues and spontaneous activity establish the initial architecture.

Early visual experience

Correlated sensory activity refines and stabilizes connections.

Critical period

Experience can produce especially large changes in circuit organization.

Mature cortex

Plasticity persists, but increasing inhibitory and structural constraints favor stability.

The central idea

The visual system is neither completely predetermined nor constructed from scratch by experience.

Development creates an organized initial circuit.

Activity and sensory experience refine that circuit.

Critical periods regulate when experience has its strongest effects.

The mature visual system is therefore the product of an interaction between developmental programs, spontaneous activity, sensory experience and plasticity.
